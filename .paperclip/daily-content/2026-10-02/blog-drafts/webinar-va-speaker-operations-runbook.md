# Write a speaker-operations runbook for a webinar virtual assistant

A webinar can fail before anyone presses the broadcast button. A speaker submits the wrong slide deck, captions are unassigned, the host and producer use different start times, or nobody knows whether the recording may be published. A virtual assistant can control the operating record. The event owner retains speaker selection, editorial approval, consent decisions, and final publication authority.

The runbook should connect every task to a deadline, source, owner, and fallback. It should let the production team see what is ready without treating a checked logistics box as approval of the presentation itself.

## Define the production clock

Work backward from the public start time in a named time zone. Add deadlines for speaker confirmation, biography and headshot, topic approval, slide review, accessibility materials, technical rehearsal, final deck, platform setup, reminder messages, recording decision, and replay review. Show the speaker's zone beside every live appointment.

Separate a target date from a hard cutoff. A biography may be useful two weeks ahead while a platform import may have a strict deadline. Name who can accept a late item and what downstream work it affects. The assistant tracks the clock but does not waive editorial or accessibility review.

Use one event identifier across calendar invitations, files, platform records, and the run sheet. Avoid subject lines such as "final final webinar" that make revisions difficult to trace.

## Collect accessible speaker assets

Create an asset register with title, speaker name and pronunciation, role, approved biography, headshot source and permission state, slide-deck version, alt-text status, transcript or notes, contact channel, and reviewer. Link files in controlled storage rather than attaching copies to several email threads.

The W3C Web Accessibility Initiative provides guidance for making audio and video media accessible, including captions, transcripts, and audio description considerations. The event owner should define the standard that applies. The assistant can collect inputs, identify missing items, and route them to the accessibility or production reviewer.

Do not invent alt text for a complex chart without the speaker or subject owner confirming its meaning. A description should communicate the point of the visual, not merely list its colors. Flag fast animations, tiny text, uncaptioned clips, and image-only slides for review.

## Rehearse failure paths

A rehearsal is not just a sound check. Test the exact platform role for each person, entry link, screen sharing, slide advance, video playback, captions, audience questions, recording indicator, and backup contact. Confirm who takes over if the host, speaker, or producer disconnects.

Use a synthetic disruption. Ask the speaker to reconnect, switch microphones, or send the deck to the producer. Confirm that the assistant knows which action is permitted. The assistant may resend a verified link and notify the backup host. The assistant should not publish an unapproved recording or change the speaker's content to fill time.

Record the test result by function. "Rehearsal complete" hides whether captions failed while audio worked. Open defects need an owner and retest time.

## Work a two-speaker example

Suppose a webinar includes a customer operations leader in Manila and a product manager in California. The invitation lists 9:00 a.m. Pacific time, while the event page lists midnight in Manila. One speaker's deck includes a video with music and no captions.

The assistant confirms the event's controlling time and corrects the inconsistent display after owner approval. The asset register links both deck versions and marks the video as awaiting rights and accessibility review. The producer decides to replace it with an approved captioned clip.

During rehearsal, the California speaker cannot share the correct window. The assistant follows the fallback: the producer shares the approved deck while the speaker cues slide changes. The run sheet records this arrangement. Nobody assumes the original problem will disappear during the live event.

## Control recording consent and publication

Record the approved consent or release state for each speaker and participant class. A calendar acceptance is not automatically recording consent. Questions about editing, reuse, licensing, confidentiality, or withdrawal go to the authorized event owner or adviser.

Before broadcast, the host confirms whether recording is enabled and which notice the audience receives. After the event, preserve the source recording in restricted storage. The assistant can prepare a replay package with title, description, captions, transcript, thumbnail source, and links. The owner approves publication.

If a speaker requests a correction, record the exact request and route it. Do not promise removal or make a substantive edit without authority.

## Close captions, replay, and evidence

Review the caption file against the audio, especially names, technical terms, numbers, and questions. Confirm that the replay page has the correct title, speaker details, canonical URL, image permission, and accessible player controls. Check the actual public asset after approval rather than assuming a successful upload is visible.

The closeout record should show attendance source, recording location, published URL if approved, caption version, unresolved corrections, consent evidence, and retention owner. Do not publish private chat, attendee details, or backstage notes as part of a replay package.

Pilot the runbook on a small internal session with a remote speaker, prerecorded clip, caption workflow, reconnection test, and a deliberate late asset. Review every exception before using it for a public event.

Run the closeout as part of the pilot instead of ending when the session stops. Produce a short replay candidate, send the caption file through review, replace one misspelled name, and verify that the corrected file reaches the player. Confirm that a withdrawn draft cannot remain public through an old link. The exercise shows whether production ownership continues after broadcast and whether the team can correct a visible error without losing the source recording, consent record, or earlier caption version.

See the [W3C media accessibility guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/) for background. Apply relevant accessibility, privacy, copyright, recording, and platform requirements with qualified owners.
